# Dataset Description

## Overview

Two things are combined here. The first is a corpus of real news articles with manually assigned categories. The second is a measured record of how one small open-weight text classifier responds when the same article is put to it under 256 systematically different prompts.

The article corpus is MN-DS, a collection of 10,917 news articles published between January 2019 and December 2019 and gathered from 44 news sources. Every article was labelled by hand against a two-level taxonomy derived from the IPTC Media Topic standard, giving 17 top-level and 109 second-level categories. The articles are real journalism with real editorial labels; none of the text was generated.

The response record was produced for this task. A stratified sample of articles was drawn from the corpus, sixteen prompt components were defined, and every one of the 256 ordered component pairs was applied to every sampled article. Each of those prompts was put to the classifier once and its full 17-way answer distribution was recorded. The measurements are the dataset's second half and they exist nowhere else, so no amount of retrieval substitutes for modelling them.

The article text, headlines and category labels here are that published corpus, unmodified apart from the truncation described below. The response measurements were generated by the task author.

## File Structure

This dataset upload contains exactly five files at the top level:

- atlas.csv - the measured response record. One row per article and ordered component pair.
- articles.csv - the sampled news articles, with the exact text the classifier was shown.
- components.csv - the sixteen prompt components.
- LICENSE_DATA.txt - the corpus licence and citation for the underlying news corpus.
- solution.ipynb - the reference solution notebook for the task built on this dataset. It is documentation, not data: nothing in it is read by prepare.py or by the grader. It is included here because it shows how the three data files are meant to be joined and used, and it runs end to end against the prepared public files.

The prepare.py script submitted with the task builds the article-disjoint train and test split from these four files, publishes the training articles' full response surface to solvers, and routes the test articles' surface to the private grading key.

## Features

### atlas.csv

- article_id (string): joins to articles.csv.
- comp_a (string): the component applied first. C01 through C16.
- comp_b (string): the component applied second. C01 through C16.
- p_arts, p_conflict, p_crime, p_disaster, p_economy, p_education, p_environment, p_health, p_human, p_labour, p_lifestyle, p_politics, p_religion, p_science, p_society, p_sport, p_weather (float): the classifier's probability on each of the seventeen top-level categories under that ordered pair. The seventeen values sum to 1.

Every article contributes exactly 256 rows, one per ordered pair, including the sixteen pairs in which a component is applied twice.

Two of the sixteen components change the order in which the answer options are listed, which changes which option letter each category receives. The recorded probabilities are already folded back onto categories rather than letters, so a value in the p_sport column always refers to the sport category regardless of which letter it carried in that prompt.

### articles.csv

- article_id (string): identifier, taken from the corpus.
- title (string): the article headline, as published.
- text (string): the article body as shown to the classifier, truncated to the prompt's character budget.
- true_category (string): the human-assigned top-level category from the corpus.
- source (string): the publication the article came from.
- date (string): the publication date.

Articles were sampled stratified by top-level category so that all seventeen categories appear, and were restricted to those with a headline of 20 to 200 characters and a body of at least 400 characters, so that every prompt variant has enough text to act on.

### components.csv

- component_id (string): C01 through C16.
- name (string): a short handle, for example expert_role or three_shot.
- description (string): what the component does to the prompt.

The sixteen components are ordinary prompt edits: state a role, name the taxonomy, add one or three worked examples, ask the model to consider the subject first, demand a single-letter answer, forbid hedging, reverse the option order, sort the options alphabetically, show only the headline, put the body before the headline, cut the article short, warn about a commonly confused pair of categories, name a category that is unlikely, ask for the most probable label under uncertainty, and strip the framing to a bare label request. None of them is a jailbreak, and none of them names or hints at the correct answer.

Each component is written as a pure function on the prompt's parts and is idempotent, which is what makes an ordered pair well defined and lets a component be paired with itself.

## How the measurements were made

Every prompt ends with the seventeen categories listed as lettered options and the token Answer followed by a colon. A single forward pass gives the model's next-token distribution; that distribution is restricted to the seventeen option letters and renormalised, which yields the full 17-way answer distribution from one pass rather than from repeated sampling. The measurement is therefore deterministic: re-running the same prompt gives the same numbers, with no temperature and no sampling noise.

The classifier is a small open-weight instruction-tuned model under a permissive licence. Its identity is not published with the task, because a solver who identified it could in principle run it themselves rather than model its behaviour from the training atlas, which is the actual problem being posed.

## Split Design

prepare.py splits by ARTICLE, not by row. Every article is assigned wholly to train or to test, so no test article's response surface is visible anywhere in the public files. Roughly 62 percent of articles become training.

Each article contributes three items, one per target category. Target categories are drawn from the categories the classifier does NOT already pick by default for that article, so every item genuinely requires steering rather than rewarding a solver who leaves the prompt alone. The draw is seeded from the article id, so it is reproducible and independent of row order.

A row-level random split would put an article's own response surface on both sides and reduce the task to lookup, so it is not used. The article-disjoint split forces a solver to learn how the components behave as a function of what the article is about.

## What makes this hard

The response surface is not additive. Knowing what each component does on its own is not enough to know what a pair does, because the second component frequently overrides or amplifies the first: cutting the article short after adding three worked examples is not the sum of the two effects.

The surface is also not stable across articles. The same component pair that lifts a target category on one article suppresses it on another, so a solver who finds one globally good pair and submits it everywhere is capped well below what the task allows.

The nine required fields describe the same surface from different angles, which rewards a solution that predicts the surface once and reads the fields off it, and penalises one that fits nine unrelated models.

## Sample-Size Summary

- Source corpus articles: 10,917
- Top-level categories: 17
- Prompt components: 16
- Ordered component pairs per article: 256
- Target categories per article: 3

Exact sampled-article, row and measurement counts are fixed by prepare.py and are reported in the task's problem description.
